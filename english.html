I was born in 1883 in Teplice, in northern Bohemia. In 1904, I am 21 years old. I am a student at the Faculty of Arts, and have travelled throughout Bohemia, Slovakia and Europe. I publish short stories about my travels. Národní listy has therefore offered me the position of an unpaid reporter at the World’s Fair in St Louis in the United States of America.

After the exhibition ends, I’ll be staying in the US and travelling around Nebraska, California, Texas and Arizona. There, I’ll be documenting the lives of my compatriots.

I was championing the idea of an independent Czech state, and as a result I became acquainted with the Herman family from Los Angeles. And it was this very family that proved to be my undoing. For I fell in love with Adolf Herman’s daughter, Elsie Herman. In 1910, I married Elsie.

We’re setting off on our honeymoon to the Pacific that very same year for six months.

In 1912, we set sail on a round-the-world voyage. Over the course of the year, we travelled through Japan, China, Malaysia, Vietnam, India and a large part of Europe. The material gathered, particularly in Japan, later formed the basis for the books *The Shattered Rainbow* and *Ghosts and Miracles*. We returned to Prague in 1913.

I then undertook a year-long lecture tour of Bohemia, turning our travel experiences into a literary work. I also began studying natural sciences at Charles University. Politically, I continued to campaign for the establishment of an independent Czech state. In 1916, I was sentenced to a year’s imprisonment for an anti-Austrian pamphlet.

A lecture tour of Bohemia and Moravia

Following the establishment of an independent Czechoslovakia in 1918, I was appointed a member of the Czechoslovak delegation to the peace negotiations in Paris. In 1920, my wife and I left for Brazil for four years. There, I was appointed extraordinary envoy. However, I did not give up writing novels. I also became a great admirer of Brazilian nature.

Ambassador Extraordinary and Plenipotentiary to Brazil
Correspondence from the late 1920s

The Opium Commission, established by the League of Nations in Geneva, approached me in 1929 and asked me to become one of its commissioners. I therefore travelled to East Asia for nine months to investigate the issue of opium and its routes into Europe. This is the first time I have travelled without my wife, but I write to her every day.

A member of the Opium Commission
Correspondence with my wife

Before the start of the Second World War, Elsie and I moved house several times. We lived successively in Paris, Monaco and Los Angeles.

Before the outbreak of the Second World War

During the war, I supported the resistance movement and the government-in-exile; I gave lectures and published articles in newspapers for the Czech diaspora. In 1943, I was appointed Ambassador of the Czechoslovak Republic to Chile. There, too, I continued my work for the resistance, raising funds for the Czechoslovak Red Cross and the government-in-exile in London. Thanks to Czechoslovakia’s good reputation amongst foreign diplomats and the Chilean government, President Beneš’s first attempt to recall me failed. I was not finally recalled until 1947.

Ambassador to Chile
Documents and correspondence during the Second World War

After the war, my wife and I settled in the United States. We were both disillusioned by the post-war direction of Czechoslovak politics. I was refused citizenship in America, but I was able to stay here thanks to my wife Elsie’s American citizenship. I continue to write and travel with my wife through California’s national parks.

Following the death of my wife in 1957, I have shut myself away from the world in my home. On rare occasions, I meet up with and correspond with a few friends. I am also writing my memoirs.
